Swallowing Difficulties in Older US Adults: Higher Prevalence Among Stroke Survivors in 2025 Than in 2022

Abstract
Background: We compared reported swallowing difficulties among US adults aged 65 years and older in 2022 and 2025, with attention to stroke history and response status.
Methods: We analyzed National Health Interview Survey public-use Sample Adult files using survey weights and design-based variance estimation. The primary outcome was reported swallowing difficulty during the preceding 12 months. Prevalences were standardized to the US 2000 population aged 65 years and older. We estimated between-year differences and the difference between changes in respondents with and without stroke history. Exploratory analyses examined age and sex adjustment and restriction to confirmed self-responses.
Results: Valid primary responses were available for 8,485 adults in 2022 and 8,223 in 2025. Age-standardized prevalence was 7.77% versus 8.35%, respectively, a difference of 0.57 percentage points (95% confidence interval [CI], −0.40 to 1.55). Among respondents with stroke history, prevalence was 14.15% versus 20.43%, a difference of 6.27 points (95% CI, 1.08 to 11.47). The additive interaction was 6.05 points (95% CI, 0.77 to 11.33). The stroke-group difference remained positive after age and sex adjustment but decreased to 4.53 points (95% CI, −0.42 to 9.48) with self-response restriction.
Conclusions: The overall comparison did not establish a clear prevalence difference. A larger increase was observed among respondents with stroke history, but its magnitude and precision were sensitive to response-status restriction. Changes in survival after severe stroke, survivor composition or symptom reporting are possible explanations requiring dedicated study.

Background
Dysphagia refers to difficulty swallowing and can compromise the safe and efficient intake of food, liquids, and medication. It is associated with malnutrition, pneumonia, impaired medication intake, higher hospitalization costs, and increased mortality [1–3]. Older adults may be particularly vulnerable to these consequences because age-related changes in swallowing, termed presbyphagia, can reduce functional reserve even in otherwise healthy individuals [1, 4]. This reduced reserve may be further challenged by neurological conditions such as stroke, which become more common with advancing age [1]. Together, these factors provide the clinical rationale for focusing on adults aged 65 years and older. 
National Health Interview Survey (NHIS) is a continuous household survey conducted by the National Center for Health Statistics in the United States of America. It uses a multistage probability sample and interviews conducted by trained Census Bureau staff, in person or by telephone, to collect primarily self-reported health information. Survey weights are designed to produce nationally representative estimates for the civilian noninstitutionalized US population, and de-identified public-use data and documentation are freely available [5, 6].
Swallowing difficulties are relevant to population health because they occur beyond hospitals and specialist clinics. In the 2012 NHIS, approximately 4.0% of US adults reported a swallowing problem during the preceding year, and relatively few affected respondents sought professional care [7]. A subsequent comparison of NHIS 2012 and 2022 reported an increase in all-adult prevalence from 4.02% to 5.91% [8]. Whether a similar difference is apparent in more recent survey years, particularly among older adults, requires separate assessment.
Estimates among community-dwelling older adults vary with symptom definitions, recall periods, sampling and assessment methods. Reviews show considerable heterogeneity, making pooled prevalence an imperfect benchmark for an individual survey [9, 10]. A US National Health and Aging Trends Study (NHATS) analysis reported annual weighted prevalence between 8.7% and 10.2% across 11 rounds, with symptoms often reported intermittently [11]. Such observations support repeated surveillance while cautioning against interpreting separate cross-sectional estimates as individual deterioration or recovery.
Stroke is a major cause of dysphagia [12], but findings from acute stroke care do not directly describe the burden among community-dwelling stroke survivors. In the acute setting, prevalence estimates depend strongly on whether swallowing is evaluated by screening, clinical examination, or instrumental assessment [12, 13]. Swallowing function may subsequently improve, although difficulties can persist beyond the acute phase [14]. Clinical guidance therefore emphasizes recognition, assessment, and management of post-stroke dysphagia through nutritional support, oral hygiene, and individualized swallowing rehabilitation [12, 15, 16]. However, recommendations for structured long-term reassessment remain limited [17], highlighting the relevance of examining swallowing difficulties among survivors living in the community. 
Against this background, we compared the prevalence of reported swallowing difficulties among US adults aged 65 years and older in NHIS 2022 and 2025, using a common age standard. Stroke was selected for a separate subgroup analysis because of its clinical relevance and because a history of physician-diagnosed stroke was recorded in both survey years, allowing this group to be defined consistently. The study plan specified a comparison within this subgroup and an assessment of whether the between-year difference varied between respondents with and without a stroke history. Secondary objectives were to characterize substantial swallowing difficulty and prolonged symptoms. Additional exploratory analyses assessed whether the findings were sensitive to age and sex adjustment or restriction to confirmed self-responses. These comparisons were intended to describe population-level differences between the two survey years and were not designed to establish their causes.

Methods
Study design and population
We conducted a repeated cross-sectional analysis of the 2022 and 2025 NHIS public-use Sample Adult files. NHIS samples the civilian noninstitutionalized US population [5, 6]. Adults with known age of at least 65 years were eligible; the highest public-use age value represents 85 years or older. The two files provide distinct cross-sectional observations and do not support individual longitudinal follow-up.
Survey comparability
The swallowing items and stroke-history item had matching wording, universes and response codes. Nevertheless, a new sampling design began in 2025, and survey operations were disrupted by a lapse in federal appropriations. Collection was paused from October 1 through November 16 and restarted on November 18. October and November sample interviewing was delayed; the December sample was fielded in January 2026 and excluded from the 2025 file. The annual file therefore contains 11 sample months with adjusted weights [6]. The altered end-of-year interview-quarter code was retained in the annual analysis and not treated as missing [18, 19].
Outcomes and subgroup definition. 
The primary outcome was a positive response to VSLSWYR_A, which asks about swallowing problems during the previous 12 months and gives examples involving food, pills and liquids [18, 19]. Secondary outcomes were reporting “a lot” of difficulty (VSLSWDIF_A=2) and symptoms on at least 180 days (VSLSWDYS_A=6). These were population outcomes: respondents without swallowing problems were classified as negative, whereas missing severity or duration among affected respondents remained missing. Duration categories were not treated as exact continuous measurements. Stroke history indicated a reported previous diagnosis by a doctor or other health professional (STREV_A=1); timing and attribution of swallowing symptoms to stroke were unavailable [18, 19].
Analysis planning and missing data
Endpoint-specific complete cases were analyzed; unknown stroke status excluded respondents only from stroke-group comparisons. Refused, unknown and not-ascertained responses were not recoded as negative. Sensitivity bounds assigned all missing primary outcomes to negative or positive responses, both identically across years and under opposing assumptions. These are deliberately extreme scenarios rather than missing-at-random imputations.
Survey estimation and standardization
Analyses used R 4.6.0 and survey 4.5, with Taylor linearization, annual adult weights and the public-use strata and primary sampling units [20]. Following NCHS guidance for public-use cross-year comparisons, weights and design identifiers were retained unchanged when files were concatenated [6]. All domains were derived from the full survey design. Direct standardization used ages 65–74, 75–84 and 85 years or older, weighted by corresponding US 2000 standard-population counts normalized within ages 65 and older: 0.522495, 0.354796 and 0.122709 [21]. Standardized prevalences are comparative indexes; crude weighted prevalences additionally describe each year's population distribution.
Contrasts and uncertainty
The primary contrast was the 2025-minus-2022 age-standardized prevalence difference among all older adults. Prevalence confidence intervals used logit-transformed delta-method standard errors and t critical values; differences used linear contrasts and the full covariance matrix of the jointly estimated year-by-age domain means. Degrees of freedom reflected the relevant survey domain. Additive interaction was defined as the stroke-group year difference minus the no-stroke-group year difference. This descriptive difference-in-differences contrast was not interpreted as a causal difference-in-differences design.
Secondary comparisons and quality checks
The prespecified stroke-group and interaction analyses, secondary-outcome comparisons and sensitivity analyses were interpreted as exploratory, without multiplicity adjustment. Interaction estimation required at least 30 valid observations, 10 events and 10 nonevents in each year-by-stroke-by-age cell, with adequate design degrees of freedom. Sparse secondary-outcome cells were flagged and not interpreted as reliable negative findings. Record and event counts were checked against codebooks; standardized estimates were independently reproduced from age-specific means, and covariance estimates were checked against saturated survey models. These checks do not constitute a complete NCHS publication-reliability assessment. Reporting was informed by STROBE [22].
Interview-period sensitivity
We repeated primary-outcome comparisons within interview quarters 1–3, using the full survey design and annual weights. This explored the influence of the disrupted end-of-year period [6]. It did not recalibrate nonresponse weights for the restricted interval and should not be interpreted as a fully seasonally adjusted annual comparison.
The stroke subgroup comparison was prespecified in the original analysis plan. After inspection of the original results, an addendum specified additional exploratory survey-weighted logistic models with quasibinomial variance. The overall model included year, age group and sex; the stroke model additionally included stroke history and its interaction with year. Marginal predictions used the same fixed hypothetical reference in every year and subgroup: US 2000 age weights crossed with 50% male and 50% female within each age group. Adjusted levels consequently describe this reference population. Differences and additive interactions used analytic gradients, the full coefficient covariance and model-based residual survey degrees of freedom. Numerical gradients and model predictions were independently checked.
Self-response sensitivity
Public proxy indicators changed between years. A conservative 2022 definition required the sample adult to be available (AVAIL_A=1) with PROXY_A outside its question universe; other ambiguous combinations were excluded [18]. In 2025, PROXYFLAG_A=2 identified self-response [19]. Direct age standardization and age-and-sex-adjusted models were repeated in these domains. This restriction changes the represented population toward adults able to respond themselves, and its asymmetric classification may exclude some true self-responses in 2022. It is a sensitivity analysis, not a correction establishing that proxy responses are erroneous.
Generative AI assistance. 
ChatGPT (OpenAI) assisted with drafting analysis code, manuscript text and figure layouts, and with formatting. Statistical estimates were produced by the R scripts; numerical validation procedures are described above.

Results
Study sample
The adult files contained 27,651 records in 2022 and 24,215 in 2025, including 8,771 and 8,468 adults aged 65 years or older. Valid primary responses were available for 8,485 and 8,223, with 653 and 689 positive responses, respectively. Primary-outcome missingness was 286/8,771 (3.26%) and 245/8,468 (2.89%) unweighted, corresponding to weighted proportions of 3.64% and 3.06%. Valid stroke-group analyses included 671 respondents in 2022 and 590 in 2025, with 98 and 108 positive responses. The corresponding no-stroke denominators were 7,796 and 7,615. Overall prevalence
Crude weighted prevalence was 7.61% in 2022 and 8.14% in 2025. After age standardization, prevalence was 7.77% (95% CI, 7.13–8.47) and 8.35% (95% CI, 7.67–9.08), respectively. The difference was 0.57 percentage points (95% CI, −0.40 to 1.55; P=.248). The interval was compatible with a small decrease or an increase; the result does not establish equivalence between years.
Stroke-history comparison
Age-standardized prevalence among respondents with stroke history was 14.15% (95% CI, 11.24–17.67) in 2022 and 20.43% (95% CI, 16.64–24.81) in 2025. The difference was 6.27 percentage points (95% CI, 1.08–11.47; P=.018). Among respondents without stroke history, prevalence was 7.19% and 7.41%, a difference of 0.22 points (95% CI, −0.73 to 1.18). The additive interaction was 6.05 points (95% CI, 0.77–11.33; P=.025), indicating a larger estimated year difference in the stroke-history group on the absolute scale. Secondary outcomes
Overall age-standardized prevalence of substantial difficulty was 1.49% in 2022 and 1.43% in 2025, a difference of −0.05 points (95% CI, −0.48 to 0.38). Symptoms on at least 180 days were reported by 2.12% and 2.48%, a difference of 0.37 points (95% CI, −0.18 to 0.92). Corresponding stroke-group differences were 2.16 points for substantial difficulty (95% CI, −0.49 to 4.80) and 1.06 points for prolonged symptoms (95% CI, −2.70 to 4.83). Sparse age-specific cells made these subgroup estimates fragile; no secondary-outcome interaction tests were performed. Outcome-specific differences are shown in Fig. 2.
Interview-period and missing-outcome sensitivities
Restricting to quarters 1–3 produced an overall difference of 0.43 points (95% CI, −0.65 to 1.51) and a stroke-group difference of 5.72 points (95% CI, 0.08–11.37). Assigning every missing outcome to negative responses in both years yielded a stroke-group difference of 6.18 points (95% CI, 1.18–11.18); assigning all to positive responses yielded 5.16 points (95% CI, −0.27 to 10.58). Opposing assignments across years produced stroke-group point differences from 1.27 to 10.06 points. These scenarios demonstrate dependence on missingness assumptions rather than identifying a preferred estimate. These sensitivity comparisons are shown in Fig. 3.

Additional age and sex adjustment
Adjusted marginal differences were 0.52 points overall (95% CI, −0.44 to 1.48), 5.84 points in the stroke-history group (95% CI, 0.80–10.88), and 0.18 points without stroke history (95% CI, −0.77 to 1.12). The adjusted additive interaction was 5.66 points (95% CI, 0.52–10.80). Six respondents with valid outcomes in 2025 lacked usable sex information and were excluded from these models.
Restriction to confirmed self-responses
The primary-outcome domain decreased to 8,175 respondents in 2022 and 7,944 in 2025; stroke-group denominators were 614 and 532. Age-standardized stroke-group prevalence was 13.52% and 18.05%, a difference of 4.53 points (95% CI, −0.42 to 9.48). The additive interaction decreased to 4.15 points (95% CI, −0.92 to 9.23). Combining self-response restriction with age and sex adjustment yielded a stroke-group difference of 4.05 points (95% CI, −0.78 to 8.88) and an interaction of 3.80 points (95% CI, −1.17 to 8.77). These intervals include both no difference and appreciable increases. All four methods are compared in Table 3, with stroke-group contrasts in Fig. 4.

Discussion
This comparison found no clear overall difference in reported swallowing difficulties among older US adults between 2022 and 2025. A larger increase was observed among respondents with stroke history than among those without it. That contrast persisted after additional age and sex adjustment, but became smaller and less precise when analyses were restricted to confirmed self-responses. The combined evidence supports further investigation of the stroke-history subgroup while limiting claims about a consistent population change across alternative analytic populations.
The findings extend previous NHIS research. Previous work documented an all-adult increase between 2012 and 2022, but the current older-adult comparison does not demonstrate that this earlier pattern continued uniformly after 2022 [7, 8]. Differences in age range, standardization and calendar interval prevent direct numerical substitution. Earlier analyses also crossed the 2019 NHIS redesign [5]. Two observed years identify a between-year difference, not a continuous trajectory or the timing of a change.
The overall estimates are broadly within the range of reported symptoms in recent US older-adult survey research, including NHATS, but these sources should not be combined into a single prevalence series [11]. Community studies from Japan, the United Kingdom and Korea used different questionnaires or clinical screening, age distributions and exclusions [23–25]. The UK questionnaire study, for example, excluded participants with a history of neurological disease [24]. Reviews' wide ranges and marked heterogeneity reinforce the importance of matching the measurement and target population when interpreting prevalence [9, 10].
The stroke-history finding is clinically relevant but cannot be interpreted as an increase in acute post-stroke dysphagia. NHIS respondents are survivors living outside institutions, with unknown time since stroke, stroke severity and recurrence. Symptoms may predate stroke or have another cause. Acute-stroke studies use bedside or instrumental assessments and commonly report higher frequencies; longitudinal clinical observations also show that diet recovery and residual swallowing abnormalities need not coincide [12–15]. The survey therefore complements clinical surveillance without measuring the same outcome or population.
Several untested explanations could contribute to the higher stroke-group estimate. Expanded access to acute reperfusion therapy may change the composition of the survivor population. In US inpatient data, thrombectomy use increased and in-hospital mortality decreased after extension of the treatment window, although those observations preceded our comparison [26]. More recently, TENSION demonstrated functional and survival benefits of thrombectomy in selected patients with large established infarcts [27]. If more people survive severe strokes with residual swallowing impairment and return to community living, symptom prevalence among community-dwelling survivors could rise even while acute care improves. Treatment may also reduce residual disability, so higher thrombectomy use does not itself predict more dysphagia. Differences in comorbidity, time since stroke, symptom recognition, or reporting are alternative hypotheses. NHIS lacks treatment and detailed stroke-severity data, so these mechanisms cannot be tested or attributed to the observed difference.
The response-status analysis is particularly informative because restrictions on who can answer may change the composition of a stroke-survivor sample. The decrease in the point estimate after excluding proxy and ambiguous responses could reflect differences in health, functional ability, reporting or classification. It cannot establish that proxy reports inflated the original result. The strict 2022 harmonization also differs from the direct 2025 flag [18, 19]. A confidence interval crossing zero after restriction does not prove absence of a difference; conversely, the primary interval excluding zero does not establish robustness to all plausible selection mechanisms.
Reported symptoms matter even without diagnostic confirmation. NHIS 2022 analyses linked swallowing complaints with functional limitations, while NHATS analyses found associations with psychosocial health, food insecurity and being homebound [28–30]. These studies provide context for potential service needs, although they are observational and some use overlapping source populations. Prospective community evidence links clinically assessed swallowing impairment with subsequent respiratory infection [31]. Such findings justify attention to symptom burden but should not be treated as complications demonstrated in the present respondents.
Care access remains a separate issue. Previous NHIS work identified disparities in reported swallowing problems and treatment, and a 2012/2022 comparison did not establish a corresponding clear improvement in treatment access [32, 33]. Our analysis did not test care utilization, treatment effectiveness or policy effects. Consequently, the stroke-group difference cannot be attributed to reduced access, inadequate rehabilitation or a specific policy. Those explanations require studies with appropriately measured exposures and temporal information.
Several limitations constrain inference. The single symptom question does not distinguish oropharyngeal from esophageal disorders, identify aspiration, or establish clinical severity. Validation of a multi-item instrument such as EAT-10 does not validate the NHIS item [34]. Twelve-month recall, recognition of symptoms and proxy observation may affect reporting. Missing outcomes were handled transparently, but neither complete-case analysis nor extreme bounds remove selection bias. Exclusion of institutionalized adults is particularly relevant to older people with severe disability, and weighting cannot guarantee correction of all differential nonresponse [5, 6].
The 2025 collection interruption creates an additional comparability limitation. Adjusted annual weights, the delayed October and November samples, and the absence of the December sample do not recreate uninterrupted collection [6]. Interview-period restriction gave a similar directional stroke-group estimate, but used annual weights and did not address all participation or seasonal effects. Age standardization and the exploratory sex-adjusted models address selected compositional differences, not unmeasured changes in morbidity or reporting. Multiple exploratory comparisons, modest stroke-group event counts and fragile secondary cells further favor cautious interpretation over isolated significance claims.
Strengths include population-based sampling, identical outcome coding, explicit preservation of the public-use survey design, a common age standard and reproducible covariance calculations. Future work could assess additional survey waves, clinically characterized community stroke cohorts and consistent response-status measures. Such work would help distinguish persistent symptom changes from differences in survey participation and survivor composition.
Conclusions
Reported swallowing difficulties did not show a clearly established overall prevalence difference among US adults aged 65 years and older between 2022 and 2025. A larger increase was observed among respondents with stroke history, with attenuation and wider uncertainty under confirmed-self-response restriction. The findings support continued surveillance of swallowing symptoms in older stroke survivors while requiring caution about survey comparability, reporting differences and any inference concerning clinically confirmed dysphagia. A changing survivor population after advances in acute stroke care and differences in symptom reporting are plausible, untested explanations for the stroke-group finding.

Figure legends
Fig. 1 Reported swallowing difficulties among adults aged 65 years and older in 2022 and 2025. Points indicate survey-weighted prevalence standardized to the US 2000 age distribution; horizontal lines indicate 95% confidence intervals. Circles denote 2022 and squares denote 2025. Event counts and valid-response denominators are unweighted and therefore do not reproduce weighted percentages. The overall group includes unknown stroke status and overlaps both stroke-history subgroups. Estimates account for the survey design. Source: National Health Interview Survey public-use data
Fig. 2 Absolute prevalence differences between 2025 and 2022 by outcome and stroke history. Points indicate age-standardized differences in percentage points; horizontal lines show 95% confidence intervals. The dashed zero line denotes no difference. Substantial difficulty represents the response “a lot”; prolonged symptoms represent at least 180 days during the preceding year. Both secondary outcomes use population denominators, with valid primary-negative responses coded as negative and missing follow-up responses retained as missing. Open circles identify stroke-group contrasts with fewer than ten events in at least one underlying year-by-age cell. Secondary and subgroup analyses are exploratory without multiplicity adjustment. Source: National Health Interview Survey public-use data
Fig. 3 Sensitivity to interview period and missing responses. Points and horizontal lines indicate age-standardized prevalence differences (2025 minus 2022) and 95% confidence intervals. The dashed line denotes no difference. Restriction to interview quarters 1–3 retains annual weights without recalibration. The two extreme scenarios assign all missing primary responses to yes in 2022 and no in 2025, or vice versa. Their intervals represent sampling uncertainty conditional on each assignment and do not form a confidence band across missing-data mechanisms. These exploratory scenarios do not address complete interview nonparticipation or unknown age and stroke status. Source: National Health Interview Survey public-use data
Fig. 4 Stroke-group prevalence differences across analysis methods. Points and horizontal lines show 2025-minus-2022 differences in reported swallowing difficulties and 95% confidence intervals; the dashed line denotes no difference. The primary result is shown by a square and added analyses by circles. Direct estimates use the US 2000 age distribution. Model-adjusted estimates additionally use a fixed 50:50 male:female distribution within age groups. Restriction to confirmed self-responses excludes proxy and ambiguous response status using conservative year-specific classifications and changes the target population. The directly age-standardized stroke comparison was prespecified; the age- and sex-adjusted and self-response analyses were added after inspection of the original results. All subgroup and sensitivity comparisons are exploratory without multiplicity adjustment. Source: National Health Interview Survey public-use data
