Evidence synthesis for an English manuscript comparing reported swallowing difficulties in NHIS 2022 and 2025
Prepared 6 November 2026. Bracketed keys refer to references.json and evidence_table.tsv.

Positioning the study

The defensible study question is whether the population prevalence of reported swallowing difficulties differed between 2022 and 2025 among US adults aged 65 years and older, and whether differences varied by reported stroke history. NHIS provides repeated samples of the civilian noninstitutionalized population. This design supports estimation of population prevalence and differences between survey years; it does not follow individual symptom trajectories or establish incident dysphagia, recovery, or treatment effects. The manuscript should consistently identify the outcome as reported swallowing difficulties or swallowing problems. The survey item is not equivalent to a clinician-confirmed diagnosis, instrumental evidence of impaired swallowing, aspiration, or a defined physiologic dysphagia subtype. The official question wording, reference period, permitted proxy response, codes, and eligible population should be reproduced or summarized from the relevant year-specific documentation [NHIS2022Survey; NHIS2022Codebook; NHIS2025Survey; NHIS2025Codebook].

The most direct precedent is Bhattacharyya's analysis of NHIS 2012, which estimated that 4.0% of US adults reported a swallowing problem during the preceding year, corresponding to approximately 9.44 million adults. The paper also documented limited care seeking among those reporting symptoms [Bhattacharyya2014]. These are all-adult estimates and should not be treated as the expected prevalence in an exclusively older sample. Diagnostic information reported by a subset of participants does not establish that every swallowing complaint has an identified clinical cause.

Hong and colleagues directly compared NHIS 2012 and 2022. They reported an increase in all-adult swallowing-problem prevalence from 4.02% to 5.91%, with an adjusted odds ratio of 1.461 for 2022 versus 2012 [Hong2024Prevalence]. Thus, temporal NHIS comparisons are established in the literature. A 2022/2025 analysis adds a later comparison and can focus on older adults and stroke history, but should not claim to be the first temporal NHIS study. The earlier comparison also crosses the 2019 NHIS redesign, whereas the current comparison requires careful assessment of comparability within the redesigned survey. Neither the previous increase nor its adjusted odds ratio predicts what must have occurred after 2022. The targeted search did not identify a directly matching published 2022/2025 comparison, but search incompleteness precludes a priority claim.

What community studies contribute

Population estimates depend strongly on the population, recall period and definition. A particularly relevant study analyzed the first 11 rounds of the US National Health and Aging Trends Study (NHATS), which follows older Medicare beneficiaries. Annual weighted prevalence of self-reported swallowing difficulty ranged from 8.7% to 10.2%, and reporting was often intermittent across waves [Namasivayam2026]. The article was published online in 2025 and appeared in a 2026 print issue. Its longitudinal observations help explain why separate cross-sectional prevalence estimates cannot reveal whether the same individuals improved, deteriorated, or repeatedly reported symptoms. NHATS and NHIS nevertheless differ in questions and sampling and should not be combined into a single numerical series.

Other community studies show why a universal older-adult prevalence benchmark is inappropriate. Kawashima and colleagues reported 13.8% using questionnaire screening among 1,313 Japanese adults aged 65 years or older [Kawashima2004]. Holland and colleagues reported 11.4% using a selected Sydney questionnaire threshold in a UK postal sample with a mean age of approximately 81 years and no history of neurological disease [Holland2011]. A Korean community cohort using standardized clinical assessment reported a substantially higher estimate and identified associations with stroke history and other characteristics [Yang2013]. These studies establish that swallowing difficulties occur outside hospitals, but their screening thresholds, recruitment, demographic composition and exclusions differ from NHIS. The UK study's neurological exclusion is especially important when considering a stroke subgroup.

The reviews reinforce this heterogeneity. Madhavan and colleagues found widely varying community estimates, with substantial differences in the instruments and populations studied [Madhavan2016]. A later meta-analysis estimated pooled prevalence at 20.35%, but reported extremely high heterogeneity and a prediction interval of 4.79% to 56.45% [Frequency2022]. The pooled value should therefore not be presented as the expected US prevalence against which the NHIS results are judged. A concise discussion can use these reviews to explain variation across measurement methods while giving greater weight to the directly relevant US survey studies.

Why stroke requires careful interpretation

A history of stroke plausibly identifies an older group with greater swallowing symptom burden, but it does not define acute post-stroke dysphagia. Stroke timing, lesion characteristics, severity, recurrence, rehabilitation, survival and institutional residence can all influence who appears in a later community survey. The NHIS subgroup also includes swallowing problems that may predate the stroke or arise from unrelated conditions. Accordingly, the manuscript should use "respondents with a reported history of stroke" rather than attributing all complaints to stroke. If time since stroke is unavailable, this should be an explicit limitation rather than inferred from age or current function.

The distinction matters because acute-stroke prevalence estimates are much higher and depend on ascertainment. Martino and colleagues' review reported estimates of 37%–45% with screening, 51%–55% with clinical assessment and 64%–78% with instrumental assessment [Martino2005]. These values describe clinical stroke populations, not the prevalence of a past-year symptom among community-dwelling survivors. The same review links dysphagia and aspiration with pulmonary complications, providing clinical context without establishing complication rates in NHIS respondents.

Mann and colleagues followed 128 patients after an acute first stroke and found marked differences between early clinical and videofluoroscopic assessments. At six months many survivors had resumed their prestroke diet, while residual swallowing abnormalities could remain [Mann1999]. Resumption of a diet is therefore not synonymous with physiologic normalization. The European Stroke Organisation/European Society for Swallowing Disorders guideline likewise concerns diagnosis and management after stroke, and supports the clinical importance of recognition and assessment [Dziewas2021]. It does not validate the NHIS symptom item or justify treatment recommendations derived from a survey-year association.

Relevance beyond prevalence

Recent US observational evidence suggests that reported swallowing difficulty is associated with functional and psychosocial burden. In NHIS 2022, Hong and colleagues found associations with limitations in self-care, mobility, work and social participation after adjustment and propensity-score methods [Hong2024Function]. Those findings offer context for why prevalence matters, but cross-sectional adjustment does not establish that swallowing difficulties caused the limitations. Moreover, this analysis shares a survey year with the proposed study and is not an independent replication of its 2022 baseline.

Two analyses of NHATS 2019 linked reported swallowing problems to poorer psychosocial health and to food insecurity and being homebound [Leiman2023; Jones2023]. Their questions covered problems chewing or swallowing during the preceding month, so the exposure is not identical to the NHIS swallowing item. The studies also draw on the same underlying survey and should not be described as two independent population replications. In the food-insecurity analysis, adjusted odds ratios were 1.48 for food insecurity and 1.32 for being homebound [Jones2023]. These are associations and odds ratios, not causal effects or prevalence ratios.

Prospective community evidence provides additional, more specific context. Serra-Prat and colleagues followed older independently living adults after a clinical swallowing assessment. Impaired swallowing safety was associated with subsequent lower respiratory tract infection [SerraPrat2012]. Their evidence on malnutrition requires care: baseline dysphagia was associated with malnutrition at follow-up, but the difference in incident malnutrition was not statistically clear. A general statement that dysphagia causes malnutrition would exceed what this study alone establishes. Potential mechanisms and clinical consequences should be discussed as context rather than as outcomes measured by the present NHIS analysis.

Care access is another relevant but separate dimension. The NHIS 2012 disparities study documented variation in reported symptoms and treatment [Disparities2023]. Zhao and colleagues compared 2012 with 2022 and did not find a corresponding clear improvement in treatment access despite greater reported symptom prevalence [Zhao2025]. A before-and-after survey comparison cannot isolate the causal effect of the Affordable Care Act or any other policy. The present manuscript should not imply that a change in symptom prevalence reflects a change in treatment effectiveness unless treatment and the relevant causal pathways are directly studied.

Measurement and analytical implications

Self-report measures the recognition and reporting of a symptom, which is itself meaningful, but differs from a multidimensional screening tool or physiological assessment. The Eating Assessment Tool validation paper concerns the ten-item EAT-10 and its reliability and validity in the studied populations [Belafsky2008]. It must not be cited as validation of a single NHIS question. A respondent may report problems without an established diagnosis, while another may have unrecognized impairment. Proxy reporting can add further differences in observation and recall. Where proxy status is available, a clearly labeled sensitivity analysis can assess whether the estimated comparison depends strongly on those responses; it cannot remove all reporting error.

Complex sampling must be reflected in both estimates and uncertainty. Survey weights address the population representation encoded in the survey design; strata and primary sampling units are needed for appropriate design-based standard errors. Lumley's methods paper provides a foundation for complex-survey analysis [Lumley2004], and year-specific NCHS documentation should determine how design variables and weights are actually used. When pooling years for a comparison, the analysis must account for whether design identifiers are unique across years, define the target population represented by the pooled weights, and document any scaling. Older-adult and stroke subpopulation inference should preserve the design information required for domain variance estimation.

Crude, age-standardized and regression-adjusted estimates answer different questions. Crude prevalence describes each year's sampled target population after survey weighting. Direct age standardization compares years using the same selected age distribution; for an analysis restricted to age 65 years and older, standard-population age-band counts must be normalized within that age range. CDC describes age-adjusted rates as comparative indexes rather than actual population risk [CDCAgeStandard]. Regression adjustment can additionally account for measured population composition, but its meaning depends on the covariates and estimand. Standardized predicted prevalences and absolute percentage-point contrasts are often easier to interpret than odds ratios for a population comparison. Model choices should be prespecified or described transparently, with no suggestion that covariate adjustment transforms the comparison into a causal effect of calendar year.

The stroke comparison should report each subgroup's weighted prevalence, uncertainty and unweighted denominator. An explicit year-by-stroke interaction addresses whether the change differs by stroke history; a significant result in one subgroup and a nonsignificant result in another does not itself establish interaction. Sparse cells and wide intervals should temper interpretation. Missing data, eligibility exclusions, and any complete-case model population should be reported. Sensitivity analyses should be described as such, with consistent denominators and sufficiently clear labels to distinguish an age composition analysis from an alternative outcome definition or reporting restriction.

Reporting and conclusion boundaries

STROBE supports transparent reporting of study design, setting, participant selection, variables, missingness, analyses and limitations [STROBE2007]. The paper should identify the observational repeated cross-sectional design in its title or abstract, present absolute estimates with confidence intervals, explain the source files, and make the analytic choices reproducible. The conclusion should describe the observed direction and precision of the survey-year difference and the stroke-history comparison, according to the actual results. It should not claim a change in dysphagia incidence, individual progression, aspiration, biological severity, or treatment benefit. Noninstitutionalized sampling, survivor selection, symptom reporting, proxy response where applicable, residual confounding, and the absence of stroke timing constrain interpretation. These limitations still permit a useful contribution: a carefully measured contemporary comparison of reported swallowing difficulties in an older US population, with transparent evidence about uncertainty and subgroup differences.
